# Trauma, Poverty, Stress, and Kidney Disease — Evidence Review

Research for: website copy on the stress/poverty/kidney-disease connection (mission narrative)
Date: 2026-08-06

## Question

The Jentosy Project's mission copy asserts that "kidney disease disparities are downstream of poverty, unequal access to education, incarceration, broken homes, and the chronic stress those conditions produce." Is this thesis — chronic stress/poverty/ACEs as a causal or contributing pathway to kidney disease specifically — supported by primary scientific literature, and if so, how strongly? Where does the evidence stop being about kidney disease specifically and become a more general claim about stress and health?

## Method

Primary sources only: peer-reviewed journal articles (cited to the actual paper via PubMed/DOI, not a summary), CDC/NIH/NIDDK official pages, and major academic medical center research pages (e.g., Cleveland Clinic's research news page, used only to identify and correctly characterize a primary study, not as the evidence itself). Secondary sources (news, aggregator blog summaries) are not used to support any scientific claim in this document; where a claim below traces to a source I could not independently verify as a real, checkable paper, that is stated plainly rather than cited.

Every claim below is rated **well-established**, **suggestive-but-limited**, or **no credible link found**, per the requesting brief. The four numbered sections match the four areas requested: (1) toxic stress/ACEs/allostatic load and general physiological harm — well-trodden ground; (2) stress/poverty/ACEs and kidney function specifically — the crux of the ask, and a much thinner literature; (3) APOL1 and gene-environment interaction with psychosocial stress specifically; (4) evidence-strength ratings are embedded throughout and summarized in the table below.

## 1. Toxic stress, ACEs, and allostatic load — general physiological harm

### 1a. The original ACE Study: graded relationship between childhood adversity and adult disease

**Source:** Felitti VJ, Anda RF, Nordenberg D, Williamson DF, Spitz AM, Edwards V, Koss MP, Marks JS. "Relationship of Childhood Abuse and Household Dysfunction to Many of the Leading Causes of Death in Adults: The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) Study." *American Journal of Preventive Medicine*, 1998;14(4):245–258. PMID: 9635069. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9635069/

A questionnaire on seven categories of childhood abuse and household dysfunction (psychological, physical, or sexual abuse; violence against mother; and living with a household member who was a substance abuser, mentally ill/suicidal, or ever imprisoned) was mailed to 13,494 adults who had completed a standardized medical evaluation at a large HMO in California; 9,508 (70.5%) responded. The study found *a graded relationship... between the number of categories of childhood exposure and each of the adult health risk behaviors and diseases that were studied* — meaning more categories of childhood adversity tracked with higher rates of adult disease, addiction, and risk behavior, in a dose-response pattern.

**Evidence strength: well-established.** This is the foundational study establishing the ACE framework and has been replicated many times since. It is an association/cohort design (not a randomized trial — can't be, on this question), and the original sample was a single HMO population in San Diego, mostly insured, employed adults — a caveat worth carrying into any citation, since it is not automatically representative of an underserved urban population like the Bronx.

### 1b. National ACE prevalence and disease-attributable burden (CDC)

**Source:** Merrick MT, Ford DC, Ports KA, et al. "Vital Signs: Estimated Proportion of Adult Health Problems Attributable to Adverse Childhood Experiences and Implications for Prevention — 25 States, 2015–2017." *MMWR Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report*, 2019;68(44):999–1005. doi:10.15585/mmwr.mm6844e1. https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/68/wr/mm6844e1.htm

CDC analyzed Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) data from over 144,000 adults across 25 states. **60.9%** of adults reported at least one ACE; **15.6%** reported four or more. Preventing ACEs was estimated to have the potential to reduce depression by 44.1%, COPD by 27.0%, current smoking by 32.9%, heavy drinking by 23.9%, and asthma by 24.0% (population attributable fractions — not individual risk). CDC's own authors state plainly: the analysis used cross-sectional data, so *causality cannot be established*, and results may be affected by recall/social-desirability bias in self-reported childhood adversity, and by unmeasured confounders (including childhood socioeconomic position itself).

**Evidence strength: well-established** for the association between ACEs and the listed chronic conditions at a population level; the CDC's own limitations language should temper any causal phrasing ("linked to" or "associated with," not "causes").

### 1c. Allostatic load: the physiological-cost model of chronic stress

**Source:** McEwen BS, Stellar E. "Stress and the Individual: Mechanisms Leading to Disease." *Archives of Internal Medicine*, 1993;153(18):2093–2101. PMID: 8379800. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8379800/

This is the paper that coined "allostatic load" — the cumulative physiological cost, over time, of the body's repeated efforts to adapt to stress via the HPA axis and sympathetic nervous system. The paper's framing is explicitly that chronic/repeated stress produces a measurable biological "wear and tear" that predisposes to disease, distinct from a single acute stress response.

**Source:** McEwen BS. "Protective and Damaging Effects of Stress Mediators." *New England Journal of Medicine*, 1998;338(3):171–179. doi:10.1056/NEJM199801153380307. https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM199801153380307

McEwen's follow-up review in NEJM extends the allostatic load model, describing how stress hormones (cortisol, catecholamines) that are protective in acute doses become damaging with chronic overexposure, contributing to cardiovascular disease, immune dysfunction, and metabolic disease over time.

**Evidence strength: well-established.** These are the two most-cited foundational papers on allostatic load; the mechanism (chronic HPA-axis/sympathetic activation contributing to cardiovascular and metabolic disease) is broadly accepted physiology, not a fringe hypothesis.

### 1d. Childhood trauma and adult inflammation (CRP, IL-6, TNF-α)

**Source:** Baumeister D, Akhtar R, Ciufolini S, Pariante CM, Mondelli V. "Childhood Trauma and Adulthood Inflammation: A Meta-Analysis of Peripheral C-Reactive Protein, Interleukin-6 and Tumour Necrosis Factor-α." *Molecular Psychiatry*, 2016;21(5):642–649. doi:10.1038/mp.2015.67. PMID: 26033244. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26033244/

A meta-analysis of 25 studies (18 studies/16,870 individuals for CRP; 15 studies/3,751 individuals for IL-6; 10 studies/881 individuals for TNF-α) found individuals with a history of childhood trauma had significantly elevated baseline levels of all three inflammatory markers (CRP: Fisher's z=0.10, 95% CI 0.05–0.14, P<0.001; IL-6: z=0.08, 95% CI 0.03–0.14, P=0.003; TNF-α: z=0.23, 95% CI 0.14–0.32, P<0.001, the largest effect of the three). The authors are explicit that this is an observational/correlational synthesis: the *biological mechanisms mediating this association remain largely unknown*, and the meta-analysis cannot rule out reverse causation or shared confounding.

**Evidence strength: well-established as an association**; not established as proven causation, and the authors say so themselves. Chronic low-grade inflammation (elevated CRP/IL-6) is independently a recognized risk factor for cardiovascular and kidney disease in the broader nephrology literature, which is what makes this finding relevant as a *plausible mechanistic link*, not a demonstrated one, to section 2.

## 2. Stress, poverty, ACEs, and kidney function/CKD risk specifically

This is the crux of the ask and, as expected, a much thinner and less settled body of evidence than section 1. The honest picture: there is a real, if young and mixed, literature directly measuring psychosocial stress against kidney outcomes (2a, 2d, 2e, 2f below) — but it is far from the standard of evidence in section 1, and a dedicated scoping review of the field (2g) concluded the literature is too heterogeneous for a definitive verdict.

### 2a. Stress-related psychiatric disorders and subsequent AKI/CKD progression

**Source:** Su G, Song H, Lanka V, Liu X, Fang F, Valdimarsdóttir UA, Carrero JJ. "Stress Related Disorders and the Risk of Kidney Disease." *Kidney International Reports*, 2021;6(3):706–715. doi:10.1016/j.ekir.2020.12.032. PMID: 33732985. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7938078/

A Swedish population-matched cohort study of 30,998 patients with a diagnosed stress-related disorder (acute stress reaction, PTSD, or adjustment disorder) versus 116,677 matched unexposed patients, matched on age, sex, and baseline eGFR. A stress-related-disorder diagnosis was associated with higher subsequent risk of CKD progression (HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.10–1.37) and acute kidney injury (HR 1.22, 95% CI 1.04–1.42; higher in the first 12 months at HR 1.32, attenuating to HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.93–1.40 — not statistically significant — beyond 12 months). The authors are direct about the limits of their own findings: *"The observational nature of the study precludes any inference of causality, and the novelty of our findings requires validation and expansion to other settings."* Their own sensitivity analysis (E-value) found a *"moderate probability that unknown confounders may explain completely the observed associations."*

**Evidence strength: suggestive-but-limited.** This is a real, well-designed cohort study directly measuring diagnosed stress-related disorder against kidney outcomes — closer to the ask than most of what's out there — but it is one study, in one country (Sweden, a very different population and health system than the Bronx), and the authors themselves flag that confounding could fully explain the result. This should not be presented as settled.

### 2b. Discrimination and hypertension in Black Americans (a kidney-disease risk factor, not kidney disease itself)

**Source:** Forde AT, Sims M, Muntner P, Lewis T, Onwuka A, Moore K, Diez Roux AV. "Discrimination and Hypertension Risk Among African Americans in the Jackson Heart Study." *Hypertension*, 2020;76(3):715–723. doi:10.1161/HYPERTENSIONAHA.119.14492. PMID: 32605388. https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/HYPERTENSIONAHA.119.14492

A prospective cohort of 1,845 Black adults in Jackson, Mississippi, without hypertension at baseline (2000–2004), followed through two subsequent visits (2005–2013; 52% developed hypertension over follow-up). Medium (vs. low) lifetime discrimination was associated with a higher incidence of hypertension after adjustment for hypertension risk factors (HR 1.49, 95% CI 1.18–1.89); high (vs. low) lifetime discrimination, HR 1.34 (95% CI 1.07–1.68). Stress specifically attributed to discrimination showed a weaker association that was attenuated (no longer statistically significant) after adjusting for hypertension risk factors (HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.97–1.35).

An earlier, related Jackson Heart Study analysis — Sims M, Diez-Roux AV, Dudley A, et al. "Perceived Discrimination and Hypertension Among African Americans in the Jackson Heart Study." *American Journal of Public Health*, 2012;102(Suppl 2):S258–S265. doi:10.2105/AJPH.2011.300523. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3477918/ — found the highest quartile of lifetime discrimination/discrimination burden associated with roughly 8–9% higher hypertension prevalence versus the lowest quartile, but found *no* association for everyday (as opposed to lifetime) discrimination, and the design was cross-sectional, which the authors note limits causal claims.

**Evidence strength: well-established for the discrimination→hypertension link specifically; suggestive-but-limited for any further step to kidney disease.** Hypertension is a well-established major cause of CKD (this is uncontroversial background nephrology, not something that needed new sourcing here), but neither Jackson Heart Study paper measures kidney outcomes directly — the chain from discrimination → hypertension → CKD is partly evidenced, partly inferred. Website copy should not compress this into "discrimination causes kidney disease" — it should say discrimination is linked to hypertension, which is a leading cause of kidney disease, and keep those as two separate, sourced steps.

### 2c. Socioeconomic status/neighborhood poverty and CKD prevalence — disparity evidence, not stress-mechanism evidence

**Source:** Hall ME, et al., "Neighborhood Social Context and Kidney Function Over Time: The Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA)." *PMC*, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6594400/ — and a Drexel University/SSM Population Health study of 23,692 adult Philadelphia patients finding those in lower-socioeconomic-status neighborhoods had higher CKD prevalence than those in higher-SES neighborhoods.

This body of literature (also including CDC's own "Lower Socioeconomic Status and Disability Among US Adults With Chronic Kidney Disease" — CDC, *Preventing Chronic Disease*, 2012, https://www.cdc.gov/pcd/issues/2012/11_0052.htm) consistently and robustly shows that lower socioeconomic status and poorer neighborhoods are associated with higher CKD prevalence and incidence. This is a genuinely well-established disparity.

**What it does not show:** none of these studies isolate a *stress-mediated* pathway as the mechanism. The consistent finding across this literature (e.g., the MESA analysis explicitly ties neighborhood SES to hypertension, diabetes, and obesity rates, and to access to healthy food and safe space for physical activity) is that SES/neighborhood effects on CKD run substantially through material and behavioral pathways — access to care, diet, comorbidity burden, environmental exposure — not through a demonstrated stress-biology pathway. This is an important distinction per the research brief: this is disparity evidence, not stress-mechanism evidence, even though it's often invoked as if it were the latter.

**Evidence strength: well-established** (SES/poverty ↔ CKD prevalence association) **but the "chronic stress is the mechanism" framing specifically is not shown by this literature** — it shows correlation with SES broadly, and where studies point to mechanism, they point to material/behavioral/access factors alongside — not instead of — plausible stress pathways.

### 2d. Perceived stress and directly measured kidney function (eGFR)

**Source:** Koga K, et al. "Association of Perceived Stress and Coping Strategies With the Renal Function in Middle-Aged and Older Japanese Men and Women." *Scientific Reports*, 2022;12:291. doi:10.1038/s41598-021-04324-2. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8742036/

A large cross-sectional analysis (n=70,642; the Japan Multi-Institutional Collaborative Cohort Study baseline survey) found a statistically significant inverse association between perceived stress and eGFR in men (P trend = 0.02) but not in women. The effect size was modest: each increase in stress category corresponded to roughly a 0.27–0.39 mL/min/1.73m² decline in eGFR, attenuated somewhat but still significant after adjusting for hypertension and diabetes history (β = −0.23, P = 0.042). The authors themselves flag that the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference, that stress was measured by a simple self-report questionnaire with only "fair" test-retest reliability, and that the clinical significance of an effect this small is uncertain.

**Evidence strength: suggestive-but-limited.** This is one of the few studies that directly measures perceived stress against a kidney-function biomarker (not a proxy like SES or a diagnosis code) and finds a real, if small, association — but only in men, in one country, cross-sectionally, with an effect size the authors themselves call clinically uncertain.

### 2e. Perceived racial discrimination and directly measured kidney function decline (Baltimore, urban Black and white adults)

**Source:** Beydoun MA, Poggi-Burke A, Zonderman AB, Rostant OS, Evans MK, Crews DC. "Perceived Discrimination and Longitudinal Change in Kidney Function Among Urban Adults." *Psychosomatic Medicine*, 2017;79(7):824–834. doi:10.1097/PSY.0000000000000478. PMID: 28445210. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5595064/

This is a prospective analysis from HANDLS (Healthy Aging in Neighborhoods of Diversity across the Life Span), an NIH National Institute on Aging intramural cohort of 1,620 Black and white urban adults (662 white, 958 African American; ages 30–64) in Baltimore, all with preserved kidney function at baseline (eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73m²). It directly measures self-reported perceived discrimination (racial, gender, and general/lifetime experience of discrimination) against baseline eGFR, follow-up eGFR, and annual rate of eGFR change — making it one of the few studies in this literature to connect a discrimination-based psychosocial stressor to a directly measured kidney-function biomarker (not a diagnosis code or a disparity proxy) in a U.S. urban population demographically closer to the Bronx than the Swedish or Japanese cohorts above. Findings were mixed and interaction-driven rather than a single clean effect: high (vs. low) perceived gender discrimination was associated with lower baseline eGFR across the full sample; among white women specifically, high general discrimination experience was associated with lower baseline eGFR. The study did not find a uniform, sample-wide effect of racial discrimination on eGFR decline.

**Evidence strength: suggestive-but-limited.** This is a directly relevant, NIH-funded, urban U.S. cohort measuring the right variables — but the findings are subgroup-specific (strongest in white women, not African American adults, which cuts against a simple "racism drives Black kidney function decline" narrative) and the study does not report the same effect uniformly across the whole sample. It is good evidence that discrimination-related stress and kidney function are connected in *some* population subgroups studied this way, and a caution against overstating which subgroup or how strongly it applies.

### 2f. ACEs and decreased kidney function in a U.S. national sample

**Source:** Ozieh MN, Garacci E, Campbell JA, Walker RJ, Egede LE. "Adverse Childhood Experiences and Decreased Renal Function: Impact on All-Cause Mortality in U.S. Adults." *American Journal of Preventive Medicine*, 2020;59(2):e49–e57. doi:10.1016/j.amepre.2020.04.005. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7378887/

A longitudinal analysis of 1,205 U.S. adults (ages 35–86) from the Midlife Development in the United States (MIDUS) study, with decreased renal function defined as eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73m² and mortality tracked 1995–2015 (mean follow-up 8.3 years). Adults with both a history of ACEs *and* decreased renal function had substantially higher all-cause mortality than adults with neither (fully adjusted HR 2.85, 95% CI 1.30–6.25). Notably, ACEs alone (HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.64–2.04) and decreased renal function alone (HR 1.55, 95% CI 0.44–5.41) were each *not* independently statistically significant — only the combination was. The authors themselves flag real limitations: the "decreased renal function alone" subgroup was small (n=22, unstable estimates), renal function was measured once (not tracked for progression), ACEs were retrospectively self-reported (recall bias), and the sample likely underestimates true CKD prevalence because it's a middle-aged, not older, population.

**Evidence strength: suggestive-but-limited.** This is a same-journal-family (AJPM, the ACE Study's own journal), U.S.-based study directly pairing ACEs with a kidney-function biomarker and mortality — but the wide confidence intervals (crossing or nearly crossing 1.0 in two of three reported comparisons) and small subgroup sizes mean this is directional evidence, not a settled finding. It should not be cited for a precise "X times more likely" claim; it's useful only as one more data point that ACEs and kidney function appear connected in U.S. populations, consistent with (not proof of) the section's overall picture.

### 2g. The state of the field: a scoping review found no definitive answer

**Source:** Estevez-Garcia JA, Tamayo-Ortiz M, Sanders AP. "A Scoping Review of Life-Course Psychosocial Stress and Kidney Function." *Children (Basel)*, 2021;8(9):810. doi:10.3390/children8090810. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8467128/

This review identified 19 articles (2 cross-sectional, 1 retrospective cohort, 8 prospective cohort, 8 animal-model studies) examining psychosocial stressors — low SES, racial/gender discrimination, goal-striving stress, work/home stress, global perceived stress, migration-related stress — against kidney outcomes. It found generally inverse associations (more stress, worse kidney outcomes) across the human studies reviewed, including low SES linked to increased CKD "particularly among women and African Americans," and goal-striving stress linked to more rapid kidney function decline. But the authors' own bottom line is a direct statement of an evidence gap: *"Due to the heterogeneity of articles identified, we could not find a definitive answer to our research question."* They also note a near-total absence of human studies on prenatal/early-life stress exposure and later kidney outcomes — only animal studies address that question directly — calling it *"an important gap to fill with human studies."*

**Evidence strength: this finding — that the field itself is unsettled — is the most honest single characterization of section 2 as a whole.** It should anchor how confidently the website states any stress→kidney claim.

## 3. APOL1 and gene-environment interaction with psychosocial stress specifically

APOL1's genetic risk for kidney disease (via the G1/G2 risk variants, disproportionately carried by people of recent West African ancestry) is not in question and is not what was checked here. What was checked: is there real evidence that *psychosocial or chronic life stress* — as opposed to biological/infectious/inflammatory triggers — modulates APOL1 risk-variant expression or penetrance (the "second hit" hypothesis)?

**Source:** Husain SA, Chang JH. "Searching for Second Hits for the Development of APOL1-Associated Kidney Disease" (editorial). *Kidney International Reports*, 2019;4(7):911–913. doi:10.1016/j.ekir.2019.05.007. PMID: 31317111. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6611946/

This is the field's own framing of the "second hit" hypothesis: not all carriers of high-risk APOL1 genotypes develop kidney disease (roughly 80% of high-risk carriers remain disease-free), so researchers look for additional environmental or biological triggers that convert genetic risk into actual disease. The identified "second hits" in the current literature are: viral infections and high interferon states (HIV, parvovirus B19, SARS-CoV-2), rare co-occurring genetic variants (APOL1 duplications, APOL3 deletions), inflammatory/autoimmune disease (notably systemic lupus erythematosus), and hypoxia (via HIF-pathway activation of APOL1 transcription in podocytes and tubular cells). **This editorial does not discuss psychosocial stress, cortisol, discrimination, or chronic life stress anywhere as a proposed or studied second hit.**

**Source (confirming the "environmental stress" terminology is biological, not psychosocial):** Bruggeman LA, et al., research described on Cleveland Clinic's ConsultQD research news page (published research in *PLoS ONE*, 2019), https://consultqd.clevelandclinic.org/loss-of-function-apol1-variants-and-environmental-stress-together-may-drive-chronic-kidney-disease — this page is cited only to correctly characterize what "environmental stress" means in the APOL1 literature, not as independent scientific evidence. It confirms the "stress" being studied is HIV infection's effect on kidney podocytes at the cellular level — infectious/cellular stress, not psychosocial or life stress.

**Source:** Freedman BI, et al. "APOL1 Nephropathy Risk Variants Through the Life Course: A Review." *American Journal of Kidney Diseases*, 2024. doi (via PubMed): PMID 38341125. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38341125/

This life-course review of factors modulating APOL1 risk across a person's lifespan discusses pre-eclampsia, preterm birth, sickle cell nephropathy, systemic lupus erythematosus, infections, and cardiovascular disease as life-course modifiers of APOL1-related risk. It does not identify psychosocial or chronic life stress as a studied life-course modifier of APOL1 penetrance.

**A note on a term that surfaces in this literature and could easily be misread:** some APOL1 counseling literature (e.g., the JASN Delphi consensus on APOL1 genetic counseling) discusses "psychosocial" considerations — but in the sense of the psychological and social burden of *receiving a positive genetic test result* (insurability, employment discrimination, stigma, family planning concerns), not psychosocial stress as a biological modifier of gene expression. These are unrelated concepts that happen to share a word, and conflating them would be a real error.

**Evidence strength: no credible link found.** I searched specifically for "APOL1 second hit hypothesis," "APOL1 gene-environment interaction," "APOL1 penetrance environmental factors," and "APOL1 stress," across the current second-hit review literature and a 2024 life-course review, and found no primary study proposing or demonstrating that psychosocial/chronic life stress modulates APOL1 risk-variant expression or penetrance. The established second hits are biological/infectious/inflammatory (HIV and other viral interferon states, hypoxia, lupus, rare co-occurring genetic variants). **This specific claim should not appear on the website, even with caveats** — not because the underlying idea is implausible in principle, but because there is, at present, no verifiable primary research establishing it. If a future version of this research finds a real study on this specific question, it should be added here; until then, this is a documented absence, not a hedge.

## Synthesis for website copy

**What's solid enough to state directly, without heavy hedging:**
- Adverse childhood experiences show a well-established, dose-response relationship with adult chronic disease and premature death (Felitti et al. 1998; CDC MMWR 2019).
- Chronic stress produces measurable physiological wear through HPA-axis and sympathetic nervous system dysregulation — the "allostatic load" framework is mainstream, accepted physiology (McEwen & Stellar 1993; McEwen 1998).
- Childhood trauma is associated with elevated adult inflammatory markers (Baumeister et al. 2016) — a plausible mechanistic bridge toward chronic disease, stated as association, not proven causation.
- Discrimination is associated with higher rates of hypertension among Black Americans (Forde et al. 2020, Jackson Heart Study) — and hypertension is a well-established major driver of kidney disease. These can be stated as two linked, separately-sourced facts.
- Poverty and low-SES neighborhoods are associated with higher CKD prevalence and incidence (multiple studies, including CDC's own data) — a genuine, well-documented disparity.

**What needs heavy caveats or should stay implicit rather than asserted:**
- Any claim that stress *directly causes* kidney function decline should be phrased as "associated with" and should cite that the underlying studies are small, mixed-sex, single-country, or explicitly flagged by their own authors as unable to rule out confounding (Su et al. 2021; Koga et al. 2022; Beydoun et al. 2017; Ozieh et al. 2020).
- The Beydoun et al. 2017 HANDLS study (discrimination and eGFR in Baltimore) is directly relevant to a Bronx-focused nonprofit's narrative, but its strongest effect was in white women, not African American adults — using it to support a claim specifically about racism's effect on Black kidney health would be citing it backwards. If used, cite it for the general "discrimination and kidney function are connected" point, not a race-specific one.
- The SES/poverty ↔ CKD disparity should not be described as proven to run "through chronic stress" as the mechanism — the literature shows the disparity, and material/behavioral pathways (access to care, diet, comorbidity, environmental exposure) are at least as well-evidenced as any stress pathway. A scoping review explicitly concluded the field is too heterogeneous for a definitive verdict (Estevez-Garcia et al. 2021).

**What should be left out entirely:**
- Any claim that psychosocial/chronic life stress modulates APOL1 risk-variant expression or penetrance. No credible primary source supports this. The real "second hit" literature is about viral infections, hypoxia, and inflammatory disease — not life stress.

**Single strongest, most citable finding for each of the four research areas:**

1. **Toxic stress/ACEs/allostatic load:** Felitti VJ, et al. "Relationship of Childhood Abuse and Household Dysfunction to Many of the Leading Causes of Death in Adults: The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) Study." *American Journal of Preventive Medicine*, 1998;14(4):245–258. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9635069/ — **well-established.**

2. **Stress/poverty/ACEs and kidney function/CKD specifically:** Su G, et al. "Stress Related Disorders and the Risk of Kidney Disease." *Kidney International Reports*, 2021;6(3):706–715. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7938078/ — a real cohort study directly linking diagnosed stress-related disorders to CKD progression (HR 1.23) and AKI (HR 1.22) — **suggestive-but-limited** (authors themselves say causality can't be inferred).

3. **APOL1 and psychosocial stress:** Husain SA, Chang JH. "Searching for Second Hits for the Development of APOL1-Associated Kidney Disease." *Kidney International Reports*, 2019;4(7):911–913. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6611946/ — the field's own account of what's actually been studied as a "second hit," and psychosocial stress is not on that list — **no credible link found.**
